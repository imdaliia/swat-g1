What Are Dark Patterns?

How deceptive interface design pushes people towards decisions they did not intend to make.

By Boukhezna Mohamed Chaker Part 1 of 6

When a website makes the choice for you

Imagine that you want to buy a product for nine dollars. You add it to your basket, enter your details and only at the last screen discover an extra fee. You can still leave, but you have already spent time completing the order. Another website might make a subscription very easy to start but surprisingly difficult to stop. These are everyday examples of what designers call dark patterns.

A dark pattern is a choice in an interface that steers, deceives or pressures people into doing something they might not freely choose. The company may gain a sale, more personal information or more time spent on its website, while the user loses control. Research and consumer authorities describe dark patterns in online shops, apps, games, subscription services and privacy notices. [1]

Where did the name come from?

UX researcher and designer Harry Brignull introduced the term dark patterns in 2010. He created a website to document examples of manipulative design and explain why they are harmful. The project is now known as Deceptive Patterns. [2] Naming the problem helped researchers, journalists and regulators discuss tricks that users often noticed but could not easily describe.

The expression does not mean that dark colours are bad or that every persuasive design is dishonest. A bright button, a useful reminder or a product recommendation can help someone make a decision. The problem begins when the interface works against an informed and voluntary choice.

A useful question

Is this screen helping the person complete their own goal, or is it mainly helping the service obtain a different result?

Bad design or intentional manipulation?

Not every frustrating interface is a dark pattern. A form that breaks on a small screen might simply be poorly tested. An unclear icon can be an honest mistake. A deceptive interface, in contrast, gives the business an advantage through the confusion it creates. For example, the site may make the accept option easy to see and the reject option hard to find.

We cannot always know what a designer intended merely by looking at a page. Sometimes a company tests different designs and chooses the version that produces more sign-ups. Sometimes the design is accidental, but the harmful effect remains. This is why it is useful to examine both the way the interface works and the effect it has on users, rather than claiming to know someone's private intentions.

Comparing two kinds of design failure
Poor usabilityDeceptive design
A button is too small to tap.The button to refuse an offer is deliberately difficult to notice.
A form contains an unclear error message.A form uses confusing words to obtain permission.
The layout accidentally breaks.The checkout reveals important fees very late.

Why does it matter?

Dark patterns can cost money, expose personal information, waste time and reduce trust. They can also make digital services harder to use for people who are tired, unfamiliar with technology or relying on accessibility tools. A user should not have to solve a puzzle in order to refuse advertising cookies or cancel a service.

They matter to HCI because interaction design is not only about whether a button works. It is also about whether people understand their choices and remain in control. Ethical design shows prices early, uses honest wording, treats positive and negative choices fairly and makes it possible to change one's mind. [3]

In the next pages we will explore common techniques, study evidence about their frequency, examine real cases and compare misleading interfaces with clearer alternatives. The goal is to learn how to recognise the trick and design a better experience.
