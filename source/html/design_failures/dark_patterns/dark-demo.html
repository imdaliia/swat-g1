Spot the Trick!

Compare simple misleading interface examples with clearer, more respectful alternatives.

By Boukhezna Mohamed Chaker Part 6 of 6

Learn to recognise a dark pattern

This page presents four small examples made only with ordinary HTML and the site's existing CSS. They are fictional demonstrations, not real services. They do not send information or create subscriptions. You can use the checkboxes and open the explanations to see how the same task can be designed in two different ways.

Try reading each pair of interfaces as if you were in a hurry. Which one makes the choices easier to understand? Which one pushes you towards the answer preferred by the website? After comparing both, open the explanation below the example.

Example 1: cookie consent

Misleading example

Deceptive

For the best experience, agree to all cookies and continue.

ACCEPT ALL COOKIES

Other options are hidden in settings.

Better example

Transparent

We use optional cookies for analytics. You can accept or reject them.

Accept optional cookies

Reject optional cookies

Reveal the trick in example 1

The first message makes acceptance highly visible while hiding refusal. The second describes the purpose and presents both choices clearly. Consent should not depend on how difficult the refusal button is to find. [1]

Example 2: newsletter sign-up

These are real HTML checkboxes. Changing them does not save or send anything.

Misleading example

Preselected choice

Complete your order:

The box has already been checked. A hurried user might miss it.

Better example

User chooses

Complete your order:

The user must actively choose to subscribe.

Reveal the trick in example 2

The first checkbox is already selected, even though the person only wants to place an order. This can lead to an unwanted subscription to marketing messages. In the clearer version, optional consent is separate, understandable and unchecked by default when opt-in is required. [2]

Example 3: cancelling a subscription

Misleading example

Obstruction

  1. Open account settings.
  2. Find a small subscription link.
  3. Read three retention offers.
  4. Contact support to finish.

Are you sure you want to lose all the benefits?

Better example

Simple exit

  1. Open Subscription settings.
  2. Choose Cancel subscription.
  3. Review the end date and confirm.

Your subscription will end on the date shown.

Reveal the trick in example 3

The first design creates obstacles and uses emotional language to discourage cancellation. The second makes the action easy to find and explains the consequences. A company can offer an alternative plan, but it should not trap users in a long process. [3]

Example 4: confirmshaming

Misleading example

Guilt-based wording

Yes, show me the discount.

No, I prefer wasting money.

Better example

Neutral choice

Show me the discount.

No thanks.

Reveal the trick in example 4

The first version tries to make refusal feel shameful. The second gives users a neutral choice. [3]

What makes an interface fair?

Three simple design improvements
ProblemFair approach
Hidden or unequal choicesShow understandable alternatives with similar visibility.
Unexpected opt-inAsk clearly and do not preselect optional permission.
Difficult exitMake cancellation straightforward and confirm the result.

These examples show that ethical interaction design does not require complicated code. The main change is often a change in the decisions designers make: clearer wording, better defaults and fewer unnecessary steps.

When reviewing a page, ask whether a new visitor can recognise the important options and predict the result of selecting one. Check the keyboard order, the labels on controls and the clarity of feedback. If a user has to fight the interface to do something reasonable, the design needs another look. A simple usability test can help: ask a friend to complete a task without explaining where to click. Observe whether they notice the cost, understand the wording and find the option to refuse. If they select something by accident, ask what part of the screen confused them. These small observations are useful because an interface may seem obvious to its creator but not to someone visiting for the first time.
