What Does the Law Say?

How European, US and Swiss rules address deceptive digital interfaces.

By Boukhezna Mohamed Chaker Part 5 of 6

Can dark patterns be illegal?

Yes, depending on the country, the service and the specific behaviour. There is no single rule that covers every confusing button in the world. Instead, several areas of law protect people against misleading commercial practices, unfair handling of personal information and manipulative interfaces. Laws focus on effects and legal duties, not only on whether a designer admits that a trick was intentional.

This page provides a student-friendly overview rather than legal advice. A design that looks unfair may require more investigation before an authority can conclude that a particular law has been broken.

European Union: GDPR

The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) protects personal data. Its principles include fairness, transparency and lawful processing. When a service relies on consent, that consent needs to be properly informed and freely given. A website should not use confusing language or manipulative design to push people into sharing more data than they intended.

The European Data Protection Board has published guidance on deceptive design patterns in social media interfaces. It describes problems such as repeatedly prompting users, hiding privacy controls and making the exercise of rights unnecessarily difficult. [1]

For example, a prechecked newsletter or advertising choice may be unsuitable where valid opt-in consent is required. The interface should explain why information is collected and give a clear route to exercise privacy rights. Cookie rules also involve national laws implementing the EU ePrivacy framework, so not every cookie obligation comes directly from the GDPR.

European Union: Digital Services Act

The Digital Services Act (DSA) contains specific rules for online platforms. Article 25 addresses interfaces designed in ways that deceive, manipulate or significantly interfere with users' ability to make free and informed decisions. Examples include giving one option unfair prominence, repeatedly demanding an answer or making termination of a service much harder than registration. [2]

The DSA does not simply replace the GDPR or consumer-protection rules. Article 25 says its prohibition does not apply to practices already covered by the GDPR or the EU Unfair Commercial Practices Directive. In practice, the relevant legal framework depends on the behaviour being examined.

The core design lesson is straightforward: do not hide a real choice behind visual tricks, and do not make a user's decision unnecessarily difficult.

United States: the FTC

In the United States, the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) can act against unfair or deceptive business practices under its legal powers. Its 2022 report, Bringing Dark Patterns to Light, discusses misleading advertisements, hidden fees, difficult subscription cancellation and tricks that encourage excessive sharing of data. [3]

The FTC has also taken action in specific cases, such as Amazon Prime and Fortnite. Those cases show how the actual wording of offers, the number of steps and the way payment controls behave can matter in a legal investigation. Not all aggressive marketing is automatically unlawful, but a misleading or unfair practice may lead to enforcement.

Switzerland: Federal Act on Data Protection

Switzerland's revised Federal Act on Data Protection (FADP) entered into force on 1 September 2023. It strengthens the protection of personal data and requires appropriate transparency about data processing. The Federal Data Protection and Information Commissioner explains that people must receive clear, accessible information when their personal data is collected. [4]

Switzerland does not simply apply all EU platform rules as its own national law. However, transparent explanations, understandable choices and respect for people's privacy are good design principles in Switzerland as well. Whether a particular practice is illegal depends on the applicable Swiss rules and the facts.

A short checklist for ethical design

Before publishing an interface
  • Show the complete price and important conditions early.
  • Use simple and neutral language for all choices.
  • Do not make refusal or cancellation harder without a valid reason.
  • Explain what personal data is collected and why.
  • Test the page with users, including keyboard users.
