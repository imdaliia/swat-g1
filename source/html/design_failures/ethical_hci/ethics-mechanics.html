Built to keep you scrolling

Not every design failure is a mistake. Some interfaces work exactly as intended, just not for the people who use them. Most of the apps on our phones are free: they earn money from advertising, so the longer we stay, the more they earn. This page looks at the small design choices that make it hard to put the phone down: features that look harmless on their own, but together remove every natural moment to stop.

By Tommaso Russo 4 min read

Infinite scroll

Infinite scroll adds new content to the bottom of the page while you are still reading, so the page seems to never end. Before it, most websites used pagination: content was split into separate pages and you had to click "next". The difference looks small, but the end of a page is a "stopping cue", a moment where you decide whether to go on. Infinite scroll removes that moment, and research has linked it to smartphone and social media addiction [1].

Aza Raskin

The designer most often credited with infinite scroll is Aza Raskin, who says he came up with it in 2006. Its origin is disputed: the same year, a Microsoft image search team filed a patent for a similar idea [1]. Raskin, an interface designer who later worked at Mozilla [2], says he built infinite scroll "before social media got going, really as a technology to help people", and explained it to Twitter, Google and other companies [3].

"Incentives eat intentions"

Today Raskin is one of its strongest critics. He sums up what went wrong in three words: "incentives eat intentions". A feature meant to save people a click became a way to keep them scrolling, because attention is what advertising-based companies sell. It affected him too: "I actually had to write software to break my own addiction".

He later co-founded the Center for Humane Technology (see the alternatives page) [2], and in 2026 he testified in a New Mexico trial against Meta, telling the jury "this is not a fair fight". He points out that on the other side there are "thousands of engineers who have done hundreds of millions of tests" on users' psychology [3].

The slot machine in your pocket

Pull-to-refresh is often compared to a slot machine: you pull, you wait a second, and you never know what you will get. Tristan Harris described it like this:

"Every time I check my phone, I'm playing the slot machine to see, 'What did I get?'"

Tristan Harris, 60 Minutes, 2017 [4]

Psychologists call this a variable reward. B. F. Skinner studied it with rats and pigeons: when the reward came after an unpredictable number of tries, the animals kept responding at a very high, persistent rate, and behaviour rewarded only some of the time was slower to disappear than behaviour rewarded every time [5]. Nir Eyal's Hook Model builds on the same idea (see the persuasion page).

Autoplay, streaks, badges and likes

Infinite scroll is not the only feature that removes stopping cues. Autoplay starts the next episode after a few seconds unless you act, so stopping becomes the active decision. A University of Chicago study on Netflix found that people with autoplay on watched more overall [6].

Snapchat streaks count how many days in a row two friends have sent each other a snap, and teenagers talk a lot about starting, keeping and losing them [7]. A 2023 survey of 2,483 young teenagers in Belgium found that taking part in streaks was associated with problematic smartphone use [8].

Notifications and red badges call you back even when the app is closed (see the home screen screenshot on the alternatives page). Like counts turn every post into a public score. In spring 2019 Instagram started testing hiding them in Canada, and in July 2019 extended the test to Australia, Brazil, Ireland, Italy, Japan and New Zealand [9].

What could change

Raskin's proposed fixes are simple: add friction back ("It's literally like adding speed bumps to a road"), click for the next page instead of scrolling forever, and no autoplay videos. He compares opposition to these changes with the past opposition to seat belts. He also warns about what comes next: "the race to attention becomes the race to intimacy", with AI companions competing for people, especially children [3].

Conclusion

None of these features is harmful by accident: each one removes a moment where we could decide to stop. The next page looks at the psychology that makes them work so well.
