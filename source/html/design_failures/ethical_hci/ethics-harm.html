The cost for users

If apps are designed to capture as much attention as possible, what does that cost the people who use them, especially young people? In the last few years whistleblowers, researchers, governments and courts have tried to answer. The answer is still debated, so this page presents both the evidence and the doubts.

By Tommaso Russo 4 min read

Frances Haugen

Frances Haugen is a data engineer who worked at Facebook from June 2019 to May 2021, in the civic integrity team. She gave tens of thousands of internal Facebook documents to the US Securities and Exchange Commission and to The Wall Street Journal. She revealed her identity on CBS "60 Minutes" on 3 October 2021, and two days later she testified to the US Senate:

Facebook "won't make the necessary changes because they have put their astronomical profits before people".

Frances Haugen, US Senate, 5 October 2021 [1]

She later spoke to the UK and European Parliaments too [1].

The Facebook Files

From September 2021 the Wall Street Journal published the documents in a series called "The Facebook Files". One headline said: "Facebook Knows Instagram is Toxic for Teen Girls, Company Documents Show". Facebook had studied the effect of Instagram on young users since 2018, and its internal research said: "we make body issues worse for one in three teenage girls". Boys were affected too: 14% of US teen boys reported negative social comparison.

Shortly after, the company paused its planned "Instagram for Kids". Facebook replied that the articles mischaracterised its research [2].

The Anxious Generation

In 2024 the psychologist Jonathan Haidt published "The Anxious Generation". He argues that teen mental health got sharply worse between 2010 and 2015, when smartphones spread, because a "play-based childhood" was replaced by a "phone-based childhood". He names four harms: social deprivation, sleep deprivation, attention fragmentation and addiction. His proposals are phone-free schools, more free play, smartphones later and social media only from 16. The book has been a bestseller for more than two years [3].

The other side of the debate

Many researchers disagree. In a review in Nature, the psychologist Candice Odgers wrote that most studies do not find a large, consistent negative effect, and that the link might go the other way: unhappy teenagers may simply use social media more [4]. Amy Orben and Andrew Przybylski have also found that the link between smartphones and well-being is weak, and some critics speak of a "moral panic". Haidt replied by pointing to experimental studies [3]. The scientific question is still open.

Laws and courts

Governments have not waited for the debate to end. Australia passed a law in November 2024, partly inspired by Haidt's book [3], and since 10 December 2025 people under 16 cannot have an account on platforms such as Facebook, Instagram, TikTok, Snapchat, X, YouTube and Reddit. Platforms that do not take "reasonable steps" risk fines of up to 49.5 million Australian dollars, while children and parents are not punished. Critics worry about the privacy of age checks and called the law a "20th Century response to 21st Century challenges" [5].

The question has also reached the courts. In 2026 two US juries found against the companies over addictive design: 6 million dollars in California, against Meta and Google, and 375 million dollars in New Mexico, against Meta; the companies said they will appeal. Other countries, including India, Indonesia, Denmark, Spain and France, are moving towards limits for children too [6].

Conclusion

Scientists do not agree that social media causes a mental health crisis. But the companies' own research, the courts and many governments show that the concern is taken seriously. And the 2026 verdicts were not about social media in general, but about specific design choices, like the ones on the mechanics page. The last page asks what designers, and users, can do differently.
