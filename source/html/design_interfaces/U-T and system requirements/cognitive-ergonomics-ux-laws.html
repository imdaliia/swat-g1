Cognitive Ergonomics and UX Laws

Interfaces should work with the way people think, not against it. A few well-established principles explain why some designs feel effortless and others feel confusing.

Designing interfaces By Maria Miteva

Mental models

A mental model is the internal representation a user builds of how a system works. People use mental models to predict what will happen when they perform an action. If the interface behaves in a way that matches the user's model, the interaction feels intuitive. If it contradicts that model, the user has to stop, think and often make mistakes.

Don Norman's work on design emphasizes that a well-designed product aligns the designer's model with the user's mental model. The "system image" — what the interface actually shows — is the bridge between the two. When that bridge is clear, users can act without conscious effort.

Cognitive load

Cognitive load is the amount of mental effort required to complete a task. Every unnecessary element on the screen, every unclear label and every extra decision adds to that load. When cognitive load is high, users make more errors, take longer and feel more frustrated.

Design strategies that reduce cognitive load include removing irrelevant information, using familiar patterns, grouping related items, and providing clear feedback so users do not have to remember what happened or guess what to do next. Barbara Webb's HCI lecture notes describe cognitive load as a central concern for interface design and connect it to decision-making and mental models.

Hick's Law: more choices, slower decisions

Hick's Law describes the relationship between the number of choices and the time it takes to make a decision. The relationship is logarithmic: adding more options increases decision time, but each additional option adds less extra time than the one before. Mathematically, the law is often written as T = a + b log(n + 1), where n is the number of options.

For interface design, the implication is practical: broad, shallow menus are not automatically better than narrow, deep ones. If options are logically organised and labelled, users can decide faster. If they are presented as a long random list, decision time increases linearly with the number of items.

Fitts's Law: reaching a target takes time

Fitts's Law models the time required to move to a target as a function of the target's size and distance. Larger targets that are closer to the user's current position are faster to acquire. Smaller, more distant targets take longer.

This law has direct consequences for button placement and sizing. Important actions should be large enough to click or tap easily, and they should be positioned where users can reach them without excessive movement. Placing controls at the edges or corners of the screen can also help, because the cursor cannot overshoot the boundary.

Putting it together

Mental models, cognitive load, Hick's Law and Fitts's Law are not abstract theory. They translate directly into design decisions: organise options so users can predict where things are, reduce unnecessary choices, make important targets large and reachable, and provide feedback that confirms the user's mental model. These principles support the usability requirements described on the previous page and help designers build interfaces that feel natural rather than effortful.
