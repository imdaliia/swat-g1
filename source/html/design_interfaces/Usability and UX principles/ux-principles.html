UX Principles

Beyond ease of use: how perception, emotion, trust and process shape the whole experience of a product.

Designing interfaces User experience Principles

1. From usability to user experience

The previous article asked whether a product can be used effectively, efficiently and without frustration. User experience (UX) asks a bigger question: what is it like to deal with this product, service or system, from the first expectation to the long-term memory? The ISO 9241-210 standard defines it as a person’s perceptions and responses resulting from the use or anticipated use of a product, system or service.

Two details of that definition are worth noticing. First, experience includes anticipated use: it starts before the first click, with advertising, reviews and expectations, and continues after the task is over. Second, it covers perceptions and responses: emotions, beliefs, preferences and behaviours, not only task performance.

Key idea Usability is a necessary foundation of good UX, but it is not sufficient. A usable product can still be forgettable, untrustworthy or irrelevant.

2. The UX honeycomb

Peter Morville’s “user experience honeycomb” breaks UX into seven facets. It is a handy way to check that a design is not strong in one dimension while failing in another.

The seven facets of user experience
Facet Question to ask
Useful Does it solve a real need or problem for the user?
Usable Is it easy and efficient to use?
Desirable Do the visual design, tone and brand evoke appreciation and emotion?
Findable Can users find the product, and find what they need inside it?
Accessible Can people with a wide range of abilities and devices use it?
Credible Do users trust it, and does it deserve that trust?
Valuable Does it deliver value to the user and to the organisation that provides it?

3. Emotion in design

People do not experience products with logic alone. Don Norman describes three levels of processing that shape how we respond to a design:

Visceral

The immediate, automatic reaction to appearance, sound and feel. It forms in a fraction of a second and sets the first impression.

Behavioural

The experience of actually using the product: does it work as expected, is it responsive, does it make the user feel in control?

Reflective

The conscious judgement afterwards: what the product says about me, whether I would recommend it, whether I trust the brand.

Two well-known effects follow from this. The aesthetic-usability effect shows that people tend to perceive attractive interfaces as easier to use and are more forgiving of minor problems, which is a reason to care about visual quality but also a warning, because beauty can hide real usability issues during testing. The peak-end rule suggests that people judge an experience largely by its most intense moment and its ending rather than by the average, so a smooth final confirmation after a long process, or a helpful error message at a bad moment, can matter more than its share of screen time suggests.

4. Visual and cognitive principles

Many UX decisions rest on how human perception and cognition work. A few principles appear again and again:

  • Gestalt grouping. We perceive elements that are close together (proximity), look alike (similarity) or are enclosed together as belonging to the same group. Spacing and alignment communicate structure before any label is read.
  • Visual hierarchy. Size, contrast, colour and position tell users what is most important and where to look first. If everything is emphasised, nothing is.
  • Jakob’s law. Users spend most of their time on other products, so they expect yours to work like those. Familiar patterns lower the learning cost.
  • Limited attention and memory. People scan rather than read and hold only a few items in working memory, which supports chunking, clear headings and progressive disclosure.
  • Response time. Interfaces feel instantaneous below roughly a tenth of a second, keep the user’s flow for about a second, and need explicit progress feedback beyond that.
  • Cognitive load. Remove anything that makes users think about the interface instead of their task: unclear labels, unexpected layouts, unnecessary choices.

5. The user-centred design process

UX is not a finishing coat of paint; it is a way of working. ISO 9241-210 describes human-centred design as an iterative cycle of four activities:

Understand and specify the context of use
          ↓
Specify the user requirements
          ↓
Produce design solutions  (sketches, prototypes)
          ↓
Evaluate the designs against the requirements
          ↓
   (repeat until the requirements are met)

At every stage, designers need evidence about real users. Research methods differ in what they tell you:

Common UX research methods
Method Type of evidence Typically used to
User interviews Qualitative, attitudinal (what people say) Discover goals, needs and motivations
Contextual inquiry Qualitative, behavioural (what people do) Observe real work and its constraints
Surveys Quantitative, attitudinal Measure opinions and satisfaction at scale
Analytics and A/B tests Quantitative, behavioural Measure what happens and compare variants
Usability testing Mostly qualitative, behavioural Find out why users struggle

No single method is enough. What people say they do and what they actually do often differ, and numbers can reveal that something is wrong without explaining why. Combining methods gives a more reliable picture.

6. Accessibility, inclusion and ethics

A good experience must be available to everyone who needs it. The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) organise their requirements around four principles, often abbreviated POUR:

  • Perceivable: information can be presented in ways every user can perceive (text alternatives, captions, sufficient contrast).
  • Operable: all functions are usable with a keyboard and other input devices, with enough time and no seizure-inducing content.
  • Understandable: content and behaviour are readable and predictable.
  • Robust: it works with a variety of browsers and assistive technologies.

Accessible design is often better design for everyone: captions help in noisy places, high contrast helps in bright sunlight and keyboard support helps power users.

Dark patterns Interfaces can also be engineered against users: hidden costs, pre-ticked boxes, cancellation flows that are far harder than sign-up, or fake urgency. These may raise short-term numbers but they destroy trust and, in many places, are increasingly regulated. Ethical UX means being honest about what the product does and respecting users’ time, attention and choices.

7. Frequent pitfalls

  1. Designing for yourself. Designers and developers are rarely typical users. Test with representative people, not colleagues.
  2. Confusing UX with UI. Screens are only the visible part of the experience; onboarding, support, performance and policies are part of it too.
  3. Relying on a single method. Analytics alone, or interviews alone, tell only half of the story.
  4. Testing too late. Problems found after release cost far more to fix than those found on a sketch.
  5. Treating accessibility as an add-on. It is much cheaper to design it in from the start than to retrofit it.
  6. Optimising a metric instead of the experience. A higher click rate achieved by confusing users is not an improvement.

8. Quick questions

Is UX the same as usability?

No. Usability is one important part of UX: the ease and efficiency of use. UX also includes usefulness, emotion, trust, accessibility and everything that happens before and after the interaction itself.

Does good UX always mean “simple”?

Not always. Professional tools for complex domains may legitimately be rich in features. What matters is that complexity is organised, revealed when needed and matched to what users are trying to do, rather than removed at any cost.

How do I measure UX?

Combine behavioural data (task success, time, drop-off), attitudinal data (satisfaction, perceived value, trust) and qualitative observation. Pick measures linked to the goals of users and of the organisation, and track them over time rather than once.

9. Conclusion

Usability removes the obstacles; user experience gives the journey a point.

Usability and UX principles work together. Heuristics and testing keep an interface free of friction, while the broader view of UX makes sure the product is useful, credible, accessible and worth coming back to. Both depend on the same habit: observing real people in real contexts, then improving the design and checking again.
