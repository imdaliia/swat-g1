Search, Filters and Findability

When a site grows, browsing is not enough. Search and filters help people jump straight to what they need.

Designing interfaces By Alessandro Campanari

Browsing and searching

People browse when they are exploring and search when they know what they want. Most switch between the two.

Browsing works when...

the categories are clear or the user does not know the right words.

Searching works when...

there is a lot of content and the user knows a precise name.

Information scent

People follow clues, like labels and link texts, that tell them they are getting closer to what they want. Pirolli and Card called these clues information scent.

Weak scent

A "More info" link next to every course.

Strong scent

"Prerequisites: Programming Fundamentals 1" shown in the list.

Designing the search box

  • Keep the box visible, not hidden behind an icon.
  • Suggest results while people type.
  • Handle typos and synonyms, so "SA1" also finds "Software Atelier 1".
  • When nothing matches, suggest what to try next.

Faceted filters

A facet is a property of an item, like semester or subject. Facets can be combined in any order. Try it: pick a semester and a subject.

Guidelines for filters
  • Avoid choices that lead to zero results.
  • Keep the active filters visible and easy to remove.
  • Use the same labels as the rest of the site.

From overview to detail

"Overview first, zoom and filter, then details-on-demand."

Ben Shneiderman, 1996
  1. Catalogue
  2. Search or filter
  3. Results
  4. Course page
  5. Enrol

Each step should keep the context of the previous one, for example by remembering the active filters.

Testing findability

Card sorting
Helps decide the categories.
Tree testing
Checks if people find items in a text-only menu.
First-click testing
Checks where people click first on a screen.
