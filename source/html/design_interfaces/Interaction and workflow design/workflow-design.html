Workflow Design

How single interactions are organised into sequences of steps, and how the work is shared between people and machines.

Designing interfaces Workflows Automation

1. From interaction to workflow

The previous article looked at single exchanges between a person and a machine. Real goals, however, are rarely achieved in one exchange. Booking a flight, configuring a medical pump or approving an invoice all require a sequence of steps, decisions and hand-offs. That sequence is the workflow.

A system can have excellent individual interactions and still be exhausting to use because its workflow forces people to jump between screens, re-enter data or remember what they were doing three steps ago. Workflow design is about preventing this.

Key idea Good workflows are judged by how smoothly a person moves from intention to outcome across the whole task, including errors, interruptions and exceptions.

2. From tasks to workflows

A workflow is the ordered set of steps, decisions and hand-offs required to complete a task. In interface design it is usually derived from task analysis: observing or interviewing real users, listing their goals, and decomposing each goal into sub-tasks. A classic technique is hierarchical task analysis (HTA), which expresses a task as a tree of goals and plans.

0. Pay an invoice
   1. Locate the invoice
      1.1 Search by supplier or number
      1.2 Open the invoice detail
   2. Verify the data
      2.1 Compare amount with purchase order
      2.2 Check due date
   3. Approve and schedule payment
Plan 0: do 1, then 2; if data matches do 3, otherwise return the invoice to the supplier.

The plan line matters as much as the steps. It captures order, conditions and exceptions, which are precisely the parts that are forgotten when workflows are designed from the “happy path” alone.

Anatomy of a workflow

  • Trigger: what starts the task (a notification, a customer call, a schedule).
  • Steps and decisions: the actions people take and the points where the path branches.
  • Information needs: what the user must know at each step, and where that information comes from.
  • Hand-offs: moments when responsibility passes to another person or system.
  • Interruptions: phone calls, emergencies and context switches that break the flow.
  • Completion and feedback: how the user knows the goal has been achieved.

Common workflow patterns

Workflow patterns in interface design
Pattern Description Best when
Linear wizard Fixed sequence of screens with next/back Rare, unfamiliar tasks with strict order (installation, onboarding)
Hub and spoke A central screen from which independent sub-tasks are launched Sub-tasks can be done in any order (settings, dashboards)
Branching The path changes depending on user choices or system state Diagnosis, configuration, rule-driven processes
Parallel / multitasking Several tasks progress at once, with the user switching between them Control rooms, call centres, trading desks
Human-in-the-loop The machine proposes or executes; the person supervises and intervenes Automation, AI assistance, safety-critical operations

3. Sharing the work between human and machine

Modern interfaces increasingly decide who does what. The question is no longer only “how does the user operate the machine?”, but “which parts of the workflow should the machine perform, suggest or merely monitor?”. Research on levels of automation describes a spectrum, which can be simplified as follows:

Simplified levels of automation
Level Machine role Human role Example
Manual None Decides and acts Driving a car without assistance
Advisory Offers suggestions or alerts Decides and acts Lane-departure warning
Supervised Acts, then waits for veto or confirmation Approves or overrides Email “undo send”, adaptive cruise control
Autonomous Decides and acts, informs afterwards Monitors outcomes Automatic spam filtering

Higher automation reduces workload in normal conditions, but it introduces well-documented risks:

  • Out-of-the-loop problem: people who only monitor lose situational awareness and skill, and struggle to take over when the machine fails.
  • Automation surprises: the system does something the user did not expect because its mode or logic was not visible.
  • Over-trust and under-trust: users either follow wrong recommendations blindly or ignore correct ones after a few false alarms.
Design guideline When a machine takes initiative, the interface must always answer three questions for the user: what is the system doing now, why is it doing it, and what will it do next? It must also make taking back control easy and immediate.

4. A worked example: online checkout

Consider the purchase flow of an online shop. It is a short workflow, but it contains all the typical problems.

Well designed good

Progress indicator, a persistent order summary, address auto-completion, guest checkout, inline validation and a clear final confirmation with an order number.

Risky warn

A long single form with optional and mandatory fields mixed together, and error messages shown only after pressing “Pay”.

Broken bad

Forced account creation, a cart that empties on “back”, and a payment button that gives no feedback and lets the user click twice.

The three versions may have identical visual styling. What separates them is workflow design: whether state is preserved, whether errors are caught early and explained in plain language, and whether the user always knows where they are and what happens next.

5. Frequent pitfalls

  1. Designing only the happy path. Real use includes errors, interruptions, missing data and users who change their mind.
  2. Mirroring the database or the organisation chart. Screens should follow the user’s task, not the internal structure of the system.
  3. Hidden modes. If the same action has different effects depending on an invisible state, errors are inevitable.
  4. Too many confirmations. If every action asks “Are you sure?”, users stop reading. Prefer undo for common actions and confirmation for rare, irreversible ones.
  5. Ignoring context of use. A workflow tested at a desk may fail on a noisy factory floor, in a moving vehicle or with gloves on.
  6. Neglecting accessibility. Workflows that depend on a mouse, colour alone or tight time limits exclude many users.

6. Quick questions

Is a shorter workflow always better?

Not necessarily. Fewer steps usually help, but compressing a complex decision into one screen can raise cognitive load and error rates. The goal is the right amount of structure for the user’s expertise and the risk of the task.

How do I find out what the real workflow is?

Observe people doing the work (contextual inquiry), interview them, and examine artefacts such as forms, spreadsheets and sticky notes. The official procedure and the actual practice often differ, and the differences are where design opportunities hide.

How does AI change interaction design?

It shifts interfaces from “the user issues commands” to “the user and the system negotiate”. This makes transparency, explanation, control and graceful error recovery more important, because the system’s behaviour is less predictable than that of traditional software.

7. Conclusion

An interface is a conversation; a workflow is the plot that gives that conversation a purpose.

Designing interaction means shaping each exchange so that it is understandable, efficient and forgiving. Designing workflow means arranging those exchanges into a sequence that matches how people think, work and get interrupted. When the two are done well, the technology fades into the background and the user’s attention stays on the goal. When they are neglected, even the most sophisticated machine feels hostile.
