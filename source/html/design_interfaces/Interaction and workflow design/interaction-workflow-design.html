Interaction Design

How people and machines hold a conversation: the loop, the styles, the principles and the models behind every single exchange.

Designing interfaces Interaction

1. Why interaction matters

A human-machine interface is never just a screen, a panel or a voice assistant. It is the place where two very different kinds of system meet: a person, with limited attention, a fallible memory and goals that are often vague; and a machine, which is fast, literal and perfectly consistent but has no idea what the person really wants. Interaction design is the discipline that makes that meeting productive.

It is useful to separate two closely related ideas. Interaction is the moment-by-moment exchange: the user presses, drags, speaks or glances, and the system responds. Workflow is the larger structure that strings many such exchanges together to accomplish a goal, such as booking a flight, configuring a medical pump or approving an invoice. A system can have excellent individual interactions and still be exhausting to use because its workflow forces people to jump between screens, re-enter data or remember what they were doing three steps ago.

This first article focuses on interaction: the loop between user and machine, the main interaction styles, the principles that make exchanges smooth, and the models used to predict and measure them. The second article zooms out to workflows, automation and the sequencing of tasks.

Key idea Good interfaces are judged not by how elegant a single button looks, but by how smoothly a person moves from intention to outcome across the whole task.

2. The interaction loop

Almost every model of human-machine interaction describes a cycle. Donald Norman’s “seven stages of action” is the best known: the user forms a goal, plans an intention, specifies an action sequence and executes it; then perceives the new state of the system, interprets it and evaluates it against the original goal. The cycle repeats until the goal is reached or abandoned.

Goal  →  Intention  →  Action sequence  →  Execution
 ↑                                                  ↓
 └──  Evaluation  ←  Interpretation  ←  Perception ←┘

Two gaps can open in this loop, and most usability problems live in one of them:

  • Gulf of execution: the distance between what the user wants to do and what the interface lets them do. “Where is the export function?” is a gulf-of-execution question.
  • Gulf of evaluation: the distance between the system’s actual state and the user’s ability to perceive and understand it. “Did my file really save?” is a gulf-of-evaluation question.

Design work consists largely of narrowing these gulfs: making available actions discoverable (signifiers), making results visible (feedback) and making the relationship between controls and effects predictable (mapping).

3. Interaction styles

The way a user expresses intent shapes everything else. Over the decades, interfaces have converged on a handful of styles, each with its own strengths and typical failure modes.

Common interaction styles compared
Style Strengths Weaknesses Typical use
Command line Powerful, scriptable, very fast for experts Steep learning curve, poor discoverability System administration, developer tools
Menus and forms Recognition over recall, structured input Can become deep and slow for frequent tasks Business applications, settings, data entry
Direct manipulation Immediate feedback, reversible, intuitive Hard for abstract or repetitive operations Drawing tools, maps, touch interfaces
Natural language and voice Hands-free, low barrier to entry Ambiguity, invisible capabilities, privacy Assistants, in-car systems, accessibility
Gesture and multimodal Natural, flexible, adaptable to context Fatigue, accidental input, inconsistent conventions Mobile devices, AR/VR, public displays

Real products rarely stick to a single style. A modern photo editor combines direct manipulation (dragging a slider), menus (choosing a filter), keyboard shortcuts (for experts) and increasingly natural-language commands. The designer’s task is to make these layers coexist without contradicting each other.

4. Core principles of good interaction

Whatever the style, a small set of principles recurs across the literature and across industries.

Visible feedback

Every action should produce a perceivable response, quickly. Even a “working…” indicator is better than silence, because silence makes users repeat or abandon actions.

Constraints

Make wrong actions hard or impossible: disable invalid options, restrict inputs to valid ranges, and use sensible defaults.

Consistency

Similar things should look and behave similarly, both inside the product and relative to platform conventions users already know.

Recognition over recall

Show options instead of forcing people to remember them. Working memory holds only a few items at once.

Reversibility

Offer undo, cancel and confirmation for destructive operations. Users explore more confidently when mistakes are cheap.

Progressive disclosure

Reveal advanced features only when they are needed, so beginners are not overwhelmed and experts are not blocked.

5. Predicting and measuring interaction

Intuition is valuable, but interaction designers also use quantitative models to compare alternatives before building them.

Fitts’s law

The time to reach a target depends on its distance and its size. Large, close targets are faster to hit, which is why frequently used buttons should be big and placed near where the pointer already is, and why screen edges (which cannot be overshot) are so effective.

MT = a + b · log₂(D / W + 1)

MT = movement time    D = distance to the target    W = width of the target
a, b = constants measured empirically for a given device

Hick’s law

The time to choose grows with the number of equally likely options, roughly as T = b · log₂(n + 1). It does not mean “fewer choices is always better”, but it does justify grouping, categorising and removing irrelevant options from a decision point.

Keystroke-Level Model (KLM)

KLM predicts how long an expert needs to complete a routine task by summing the time of elementary operators. Typical approximate values are:

  • K keystroke or button press: about 0.2 s
  • P pointing at a target with a mouse: about 1.1 s
  • H moving the hand between keyboard and mouse: about 0.4 s
  • M mental preparation: about 1.35 s

With KLM, two versions of the same task flow (for example, a three-screen wizard versus a single form) can be compared on paper. It is limited to error-free, expert behaviour, so it should be complemented by usability tests with real users.
