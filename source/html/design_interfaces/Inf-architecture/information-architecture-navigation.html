Information Architecture and Navigation

How do you organize the content of a system so that people find what they need, know where they are, and can get back when they click the wrong thing?

Designing interfaces

1. What is Information Architecture?

Information architecture (IA) is the structural design of shared information environments: deciding how to organize and name content, so users can find things and understand where they are. The idea was made popular by Rosenfeld and Morville in Information Architecture for the World Wide Web, the "polar bear book".

Think of a building: before painting the walls, the architect decides where the rooms go (pages), how the corridors connect them (links and menus) and where to put the signs (labels). If this part is done badly, a nice graphic design doesn't save you: users still get lost.

IA always balances three things: the users (what they need and how they think), the content (how much there is and what type) and the context (the goals of the organization and the technology). It only makes sense once you know what users need to do. In our example a student has to find a course, check the prerequisites and enrol, and the structure has to make each of these steps easy to reach.

2. The Main Components

Rosenfeld and Morville split every information architecture into four systems:

  • Organization: how content is grouped, for example alphabetically, by topic or by task.
  • Labeling: how things are called. "Contact us" is better than "Customer Relationship Interface".
  • Navigation: how people move around (see the next page).
  • Search: search box, filters and how results are shown.

Most real systems use more than one way of organizing at the same time. An alphabetical list of courses is perfect if you already know the name, but a first-year student who only knows "I need something about programming" is better served by a list grouped by subject.

The result is usually drawn as a sitemap. Here you choose between breadth and depth: many options per level and few clicks, or few options and many clicks. Studies show that something moderately wide and shallow works better, because every extra level is one more chance to take the wrong path.

In the end all four systems have the same goal, findability: users should be able to guess where something is before even clicking.
