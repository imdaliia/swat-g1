Example: a Course Catalogue

Everything from the previous pages applied to a real system: searching for courses and enrolling at a university.

Designing interfaces

Here we apply everything to a university course registration system. The student wants to:

  1. search for a course and open its page
  2. check the prerequisites
  3. choose a section and enrol
  4. check that the registration went through

We picked this example because it's something we all use, so it's easy to see what goes wrong. There are hundreds of courses, they change every semester, and the users go from first-year students who know nothing yet to older students who know exactly what they want.

5.1 Organization: the sitemap

Courses follow a hierarchy (semester, subject area, course), the same way students already think about their studies. Any course is at most three clicks away. We kept "My enrolments" and "Timetable" at the top level, because students open them almost every day and shouldn't have to dig for them.

  • Home
    • Courses
      • Autumn 2026
        • Informatics: Software Atelier I, Programming Fundamentals
        • Mathematics
        • Economics
      • Spring 2027
    • My enrolments
    • Timetable
    • Help and contacts

5.2 Labels

Table 3 - Labels before and after
Internal label (bad)Student label (good)
Didactic Offer ModuleCourses
Registration TransactionsMy enrolments
Prerequisite ConstraintsWhat you need before this course

The internal labels aren't wrong, they're just written for the people who manage the system. A student reading "Didactic Offer Module" has to stop and think, while "Courses" is understood instantly.

5.3 Navigation, search and filters

A global top bar is the same on every page, a local sidebar lists the subject areas, prerequisites are contextual links, and the breadcrumb shows Home > Courses > Autumn 2026 > Informatics.

Students who already know the course can use the search box and narrow results with filters (semester, area, language, credits). Each result shows the places still available. The course page follows the steps of the task: description, prerequisites, sections, and only at the end the "Enrol" button.

Putting "Enrol" at the very top would look faster, but it would push students to enrol before checking if they can actually take the course. Filters are also just another way into the same hierarchy, so a student never ends up on a page they couldn't reach from the menu.

5.4 How we would know it works

  • at least 8 students out of 10 find a course in 3 clicks or less;
  • they find the prerequisites without asking anyone;
  • after a wrong click they get back with the breadcrumb or the menu.

If one of these fails, we don't blame the students: we go back to the labels or the structure, fix them and test again.

Accessibility is a requirement here too; the details are covered by the Web Accessibility group.
