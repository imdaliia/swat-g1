Designing and Testing the Architecture

Card sorting, sitemaps and tree testing: how to build a structure together with users and check that it really works.

Designing interfaces

You can't design a good structure just by guessing at your desk: you involve users, test and fix. Here we cover only the methods specific to IA; usability testing in general is in the next part of our topic.

4.1 Why people get lost

Table 2 - From principle to design choice
PrincipleIn shortWhat we do with it
Mental modelsPeople expect systems to work like ones they knowGroup courses by semester and subject
Cognitive loadWorking memory is smallSplit long lists into meaningful groups
Information scentPeople follow labels that "smell" right"Enrol in a course", not "Registration module"

Information scent comes from Pirolli and Card's information foraging theory: people follow the most promising trail, like animals looking for food. Vague labels make the trail weak.

4.2 Methods

  1. Content inventory: list all existing content to find duplicates, gaps and old pages.
  2. Card sorting: users group cards into categories that make sense to them (open if they name the groups, closed if the groups are given). For example, students might put "Exam dates" and "Timetable" together, while the office would put exams under "Regulations".
  3. Sitemap: the resulting structure, drawn before any graphic design (ours here).
  4. Tree testing: users get only the text menu and must find something, like Where are the prerequisites of Software Atelier I?. It tests the structure alone.

These steps repeat: test, fix, test again, also because new courses arrive every semester.

4.3 Checklist

  • Can users guess where something is?
  • Do labels make sense to students, not only to the office?
  • Is there a clear path from overview to details and back?
  • Can users get back after a wrong click?

In a tree test we measure the success rate, the directness (no going back) and the time.
